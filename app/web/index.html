01 Results

Learned representations beat hand-crafted features by 21 points.

The first approach summarised each clip as 422 classic acoustic measurements and tried five classifiers. They all landed between 47% and 53%, so the features themselves were the bottleneck. Replacing them with a frozen pretrained speech encoder and a plain linear classifier moved accuracy into the seventies.

02 Inside the model

Emotion lives in the middle of the network.

WavLM was never trained on emotion. It learned to predict masked speech from 94,000 hours of audio. Probing each layer separately shows where emotional cues end up. The bottom layers are close to the raw signal, and the top layers specialise in speech sounds. The middle layers carry the most, so that is where the classifier reads from.

03 Where it goes wrong

Mistakes stay close to home.

Most errors stay within the same arousal level. High-energy emotions (angry, happy, fear) get confused with each other, and so do low-energy ones (sad, disgust, neutral). Angry and sad are almost never mixed up.

    04 How it works

    Five steps from a waveform to a feeling.

    The heavy lifting is done by a model that already understands speech. The part trained here is one small, transparent linear layer, and it runs on an ordinary CPU.

    1. 1 · Clean

      Normalise the audio

      Converted to 16 kHz mono, leading and trailing silence trimmed, peak-normalised. Training and this demo share the same code path.

    2. 2 · Encode

      Frozen WavLM Base+

      95 M parameters, self-supervised on 94k hours of speech. It produces 13 hidden layers of 768 numbers every 20 ms. It is never fine-tuned.

    3. 3 · Pool

      Average time and layers

      Frames from layers 5–9 are mean-pooled over time and across layers, giving one 768-dimensional vector per clip.

    4. 4 · Classify

      Logistic regression

      Standardised, then a single linear layer with 4,614 weights. Layers and regularisation were chosen by cross-validation on training actors.

    5. 5 · Calibrate

      Temperature-scaled softmax

      One temperature fitted on held-out predictions, so a confidence of 80% means right about 80% of the time on CREMA-D-like audio.

    Recordings longer than ~4.5 s are also scored in overlapping 3-second windows, which gives the timeline under the waveform in the demo.

    05 Evaluation

    Tested only on voices it has never heard.

    Many CREMA-D results you'll find online split the data by clip, so the same actors appear in training and testing. A model can then partly recognise the speaker instead of the emotion, which inflates accuracy. Here, the split is by actor, and all tuning happens on the training actors.

    • Grouped by actor, never by clip
    • Layers and regularisation chosen by actor-grouped CV inside the training set
    • Each test set scored once, after every choice was fixed

    06 Limits

    What it can't do.

    Acted, not spontaneous

    Trained on 91 actors reading 12 fixed sentences in a studio. Real conversations are subtler, and accuracy drops on them.

    English, clean audio

    Noisy rooms, phone microphones, music, other languages and overlapping speakers all push it outside what it has seen.

    Six labels only

    Surprise, boredom or sarcasm get forced into the nearest of the six. It hears how something is said, not what is said.

    Not a decision tool

    Never use it to judge people: hiring, health, security, lie detection. It is a research demo that still gets more than one clip in five wrong, even in the lab.

    Your audio is converted in your browser, sent once for analysis and processed in memory. It is not stored, logged or used for training.
